# Social Niche Theory: The Fundamental Dimensions of the Human Mind

**Author:** Stephen McCranie

**Date:** November 2026

**Status:** Pre-publication theory paper

---

## Abstract

Human minds differ on a few fundamental dimensions. Social Niche Theory (SNT) rivals the Big Five: those were chosen by how people are described, and SNT's dimensions by what they predict. A fundamental dimension is one on which a person's standing, relative to others, predicts what they choose, where they end up, and whom they befriend, work with and marry. People move toward company and settings that fit them, and relationships last while both sides want to stay, so friends, colleagues and spouses end up alike.

Four candidates: general intelligence (g), how much a mind can take in; people orientation (p), whether it first reads how people stand to one another or how things work; conventionalism (c), how much authority it grants to consensus; and honesty (h), the weight it gives to others' interests. The test for each: people sort on it more than their meeting places explain, the sorting comes from fit, a gap on it ends relationships, and it is distinct.

In 81,000 Americans, intelligence correlated .52 with education, and no Big Five trait exceeded .16. Spouses correlate .44 on intelligence, .49 on traditionalism and .39 on honesty, against .1 to .2 on most Big Five traits. Neuroticism is stable and predictive, and SNT keeps it.

SNT predicts that the axes outpredict the Big Five on where a person ends up, whom they befriend and marry, and which relationships last; that people fare better in settings that fit them; and that part of autism and ADHD is mismatch.

**Keywords:** individual differences, social sorting, homophily, assortative mating, general intelligence, people–things orientation, conventionalism, honesty, person–environment fit, autism

## 1. Finding the Dimensions of the Mind

Personality psychology found its dimensions by description. The lexical hypothesis holds that the differences that matter get words, and factor analysis of those words gives the Big Five (Goldberg, 1990; Ashton & Lee, 2007). Description records what people notice and judge in each other, and the traits are then measured by asking people to describe themselves.

SNT finds the dimensions by function. A fundamental dimension is one on which a person's standing, relative to other people, predicts how their life differs from other people's: what they choose, where they end up, and whom they befriend, work with and marry. Its **sorting hypothesis** holds that the differences that matter get sorted on. People are drawn to the work, places and company that suit how their minds work, and relationships between minds that work differently are harder to keep. Easy relationships last and deepen; strained ones fade. Over years this leaves people among others like themselves, whether or not anyone chose with that in mind. Sorting is the combined verdict of millions of interactions on which differences matter.

The sorting runs through every kind of relationship. Friends are alike on 86% of the variables researchers measure, however long they have known each other (Bahns et al., 2017). Workers drift toward jobs whose complexity matches their ability (Wilk et al., 1995). Cadets placed in squadrons built to mix ability re-sort by ability inside them (Carrell et al., 2013). Spouses resemble each other in intelligence nearly as much as siblings do (r = .44, 95% CI [.23, .61]), and more still in political values (.58) and religiosity (.56). On most personality traits they correlate .1 to .2 (Horwitz et al., 2023).

The resemblance is visible in the brain, and in another species. Pairs of strangers who went on to grow closer over eight months had responded more alike to the same video clips, in brain scans taken before they met (Shen et al., 2025). Among people who already know each other, close friends' responses are the most alike (Parkinson et al., 2018). Among 38 zoo chimpanzees, friends were more alike in sociability and boldness than non-friends (Massen & Koski, 2014).

**Couples are the best-measured case.** Most of the numbers in this paper come from couples, because both sides are measured, the relationship has a dated beginning and end, and registers hold millions of them. The claim is about every relationship a person keeps or leaves, with people and with places: a friendship, a class, a job, a community.

**The outcomes are behaviour and records:** whom people befriend and marry, where they enrol and work, which of those end, and what they attain. Stated preferences are left out (§2.2).

## 2. The Model

### 2.1 How minds differ

Humans live in a socio-cognitive niche. They survive by learning, from the world and from each other, in groups that divide labour and accumulate know-how (Whiten & Erdal, 2012; Boyd et al., 2011; Pinker, 2010). Every mind in that niche has four things to settle: how much it can work out, what it reads first in what it meets, whose evidence it trusts, and whose interests count. Minds differ in each.

The first, g, is how much a mind can take in and work out. The second, p, is what it reads first: how people stand to one another, or how things work. A group divides its work between managing relationships and making tools and tracking the physical world, and it needs both. The third, c, is whose evidence it trusts: the authority it grants to consensus, the group's established way, against its own judgment. Copying is cheap and testing is costly, and a group needs copiers and testers (Boyd & Richerson, 1985; Henrich, 2016). The fourth, h, is whose interests count: the weight it gives to others' interests against its own. Honesty is the behaviour that weight produces. A group that divides its labour runs on exchange, and each member can deal fairly or take more than they give.

**The four are logically independent.** A brilliant conventional engineer and a dull autonomous counsellor are both coherent people. The four still correlate, because they interact, and the logic fixes the signs. All of the predicted correlations are within sex. g and c correlate −.2 to −.3: testing for oneself costs less for a more capable mind, so higher g gives less reason to defer. p and c correlate +.1 to +.2. To a mind that reads standing first, what others believe and approve is the most salient evidence there is. To a mind that reads workings first, consensus is beside the point: a bridge stands or falls whatever people believe about it. g and p correlate near zero, because social complexity demands as much capacity as mechanical complexity. Tests tilt the measured correlation, because matrix items are mechanistic material and verbal items social. h correlates near zero with g and with c, and about +.1 with p, since a mind that attends to people has more reason to count their interests.

Niches are discrete, and people vary continuously. A type, such as the engineer or the counsellor, is a niche: a region of these four dimensions that a role rewards. Taxometric studies find dimensional structure about five times as often as categorical structure (Haslam et al., 2020). SNT describes people by continuous profiles and treats types as properties of settings.

### 2.2 One model of fit

A person's position is a vector x. On each dimension k, the quality of a relationship between persons i and j is

  Q_k(i, j) = −W_k · (x_jk − x_ik)²

W_k is the weight of the dimension: how much distance on it matters. The best match on every dimension is a person at one's own position. The quality of a relationship is the sum over dimensions. Relationships last in proportion to their quality: good ones deepen and are renewed, and poor ones are dropped. Within any pool of people who meet, this pruning produces sorting. Deliberate choice, as in dating, applies the same quality in advance, while drift applies it through experience, and the two give the same sorting.

**What a person wants and what holds a relationship together are different things.** A person may want a partner more honest, more able or more generous than themselves. A relationship has two sides. A gap that looks good to one leaves the other short: the more honest partner gets less than they give. It costs the first as well: the abler partner is left explaining, and the less honest one distrusts what they are given. A relationship rests on whichever side is short, so its quality falls with the gap in either direction, and the relationships that last join people who are level.

**Fit works by two routes.** People are drawn to the work, places and activities that suit how their minds work, and they meet others like themselves there. That route works without any friction between people. The second route is friction: a gap between two people makes their relationship harder to keep.

**Friction is the cost of coordinating two minds.** Any joint activity, whether a conversation, a household or a project, runs on common ground: what both take as given about what is happening and what it means (Clark, 1996). Each of the four axes is a layer of that ground. A gap on g is a gap in bandwidth: one explains what the other already sees, or loses the thread of what the other says. A gap on p is a gap in code: one reads what an act says about the two of them and the other reads its content, so they care about different activities and hear different messages in the same words. A gap on c is a gap in warrant: the two disagree about what makes a claim binding. A gap on h is a gap in terms: the two disagree about what each owes the other, so one gives more than they get and the other expects to be cheated.

**A setting has a position too:** what it demands and rewards. That is the complexity of its work (g), its balance of social and technical tasks (p), the tightness of its norms (c; Gelfand et al., 2011), and how fair its dealings are (h). Friction with a setting shows as boredom or overload, constant misreading, and stifling order or lawlessness. Leaving it shows as dropout and turnover. One model therefore covers partners, friends and person–environment fit (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005; Edwards, 1994).

**Levels are held constant.** A person's own level on a dimension can also affect how long their relationships last, whoever the other person is. The model is about the gap. Every test of it keeps both sides' levels in the model, so a gap is credited only with what it adds.

**The model fits observed choices.** Hitsch, Hortaçsu and Ariely (2010) estimated what 5,787 online daters wanted from whom they contacted. The estimates had two parts: a preference for a partner like oneself, and a preference for more of a trait whoever one is. With both parts, the model predicted that couples would correlate .53 on education. With the preference for similarity switched off, leaving only the preference for more, the predicted correlation fell to .06, and to .09 or below on age, looks and income. Nearly all of the predicted sorting came from wanting someone similar.

**The evidence is the relationships people keep.** Behaviour can be read at four points. Stated ideals say what people believe they want, and they predict whom a person chooses only weakly. Across 10,358 people in 43 countries, the match between ideal and partner predicted how the partner was valued at β = .04 for a single trait, and at .11 to .19 for the whole profile (Eastwick et al., 2025). Stated ideals are left out here. Pursuit is cheap and aims up. Online daters pursue partners about 25% more desirable than themselves, and replies fall sharply as the gap grows (Bruch & Newman, 2018). Relationships formed reflect what a person wanted and who was available. Relationships kept are the cleanest record: who stays, who leaves, and how people fare where they are placed.

### 2.3 Two hypotheses: fit and competition

Two hypotheses explain why people end up with others like themselves. The **fit hypothesis** is SNT's: a gap makes a relationship harder to keep, so the relationships that last join people who are alike (§2.2). The **competition hypothesis** is the rival. It holds that everyone wants the most valued friend, colleague, employer or spouse. Where each person can hold only one such relationship, the most valued choose each other, and the rest take who is left (Becker, 1973; Kalick & Hamilton, 1986). People are ranked by their total value, so two people can be matched overall while differing trait by trait: one partner brings looks and the other brings status. That is a trade of one trait for another. SNT holds that sorting on traits of the mind comes from fit.

**The trades competition predicts are small.** Pursuit does show a shared ranking: online daters aim about 25% above themselves (§2.2). The couples that form show little of the trade, and they match trait by trait. Among 1,507 US couples, partners were alike in looks and alike in status, and once that was counted most evidence that beauty is traded for status went; a link between beauty and years of education remained, and the result is disputed (McClintock, 2014; Gullickson, 2017). Studies that control for matching still find small trades of weight for wages, social origin for education, and age for status (Chiappori et al., 2012; Schwartz et al., 2016; Xie & Dong, 2021). The trades that survive involve the body and money.

**Under fit, no place on an axis is better in a relationship.** p and c are positions, so there is nothing to compete for. The strongest sorting on a trait of the mind is here: partners correlate .49 on Traditionalism and .65 on conservatism (§3.3). A gap on g costs both sides. The abler one explains what they already see, and the other loses the thread (§3.1). A gap on h costs both sides. The more honest one gives more than they get. The less honest one expects others to act as they would themselves, and meets honesty with suspicion (§3.4).

Under competition, g and h are goods that everyone wants more of. Competition then predicts sorting on them too, so sorting alone doesn't separate the two hypotheses on these axes.

**Three things separate the two hypotheses.** The first is whether more stops being better. Under fit the best match is at a person's own level. Under competition it is always someone higher. Low-ability cadets offered high-ability study partners chose them less often than chance (Carrell et al., 2013). A pupil placed among abler classmates loses academic self-concept, and a lower class rank lowers later scores (§3.1). The second is whether gaps add or offset. Under fit, each gap costs the relationship on its own. Under competition, a surplus on one trait buys off a shortfall on another. The third is whether sorting appears where relationships aren't exclusive. Competition needs scarce places, and fit doesn't. Friends, playmates and members of a community sort on the same traits, and a person can have many of each.

**Where competition holds.** Looks, youth and health are goods of the body, and money is a resource. People pursue them whatever their own standing, and in exclusive relationships that produces pairing by rank. In the online-dating data, preferences for looks rose with the other person's looks whatever one's own (Hitsch et al., 2010). A lottery win lowers a married man's risk of divorce by 40% (Cesarini et al., 2023). Even here more has a limit: a wife who out-earns her husband raises the couple's risk of divorce (Bertrand et al., 2015).

**A gap ends relationships, and the ending leaves traces.** A gap raises the risk that a relationship ends: a couple breaks up, a friendship lapses, a student drops out, a worker quits or is let go. The ending leaves three traces: who leaves, where they go, and how gaps combine. Under fit, either side leaves, since a gap costs both, and both settle with someone, or somewhere, closer. Under competition, the more valued side leaves and moves up. Under fit, gaps on separate dimensions add. Under competition, a surplus on one offsets a shortfall on another.

**The traces, where they have been looked for,** are mostly on education, age and income. On who leaves, either spouse is more likely to leave when the other is older (England et al., 2016), and in 92 marital breakups, gaps in income and education did not predict which partner wanted out (Rosenfeld, 2018). On where they go, second unions are less alike on education than first ones (Theunis et al., 2013; Qian & Lichter, 2018), which runs against the trace. One Swedish study supports fit: the match on earnings is poorer in first marriages that end and closer in the second (Åström et al., 2013). The size of the gap matters: within religion, the risk of divorce rises with the distance between the two faiths (Kalmijn et al., 2005). Gap has been set against level in adolescent friendships. Among 573 of them, differences between the two friends predicted which ended, and each friend's own level did not (Hartl et al., 2015). The differences were in sex, peer acceptance, physical aggression and school competence.

### 2.4 The test

A disposition is a **fundamental dimension** if a person's standing on it, relative to others, predicts how their life differs from other people's. Sorting is the strictest form of that prediction, and it gives the test. A candidate is an axis if it meets four criteria.

**1. People sort on it beyond their meeting pools,** with each other and into settings. A meeting pool is the set of people a person could have met: a school, a workplace, a neighbourhood. The test is held-out prediction of observed matches (friendships, enrolments, jobs and couples) against shuffled matches within realistic meeting pools that keep the same distributions. A chosen pool counts once. A pool that people entered by choice on an axis is sorting into a setting, and counts for that axis. A couple who then meet there add no second piece of evidence. The criterion is refuted if real matches beat shuffled ones by nothing on the axis.

**2. The sorting comes from fit,** with convergence and shared background ruled out. Two designs test it. The first is a spouses-of-twins study with all four axes measured, the design that separates sorting from background and convergence (Kandler et al., 2019). The second is a randomized conversation study: pair strangers measured on the battery at random, and record their rapport and whether they want to talk again. Comparing pairs who know each other's scores with pairs who don't separates mismatch from stigma.

There is also a genetic check. Genes don't converge over a marriage. If partners sort on a heritable trait with partner correlation r_P, their polygenic scores for it correlate about r_P × R²_PGS: the phenotypic correlation times the share of trait variance the score explains. The estimate is corrected for cross-trait sorting, which inflates genetic correlations (Border et al., 2022), and for geographic and ancestry structure.

The same checks run outside marriage. In 5,500 US adolescents, friends were more alike genetically than random pairs, about two-thirds as alike as spouses, and the authors trace much of that to school assignment, which is the pool (Domingue et al., 2018). The brain scans of §1 were taken before the pairs met, so convergence can't explain them. Neither result is measured on g, p, c or h, so they support the sorting hypothesis in general. The criterion is refuted if the similarity turns out to be background and convergence, or if partners' polygenic scores are uncorrelated where the sorting predicts a correlation.

**3. A gap on it ends relationships,** beyond each side's own level, and leaves the traces of fit (§2.3). Dissolution is tested in three designs: Nordic registers for g; panels with cognitive tests for both partners; and a prospective couples study for p, c and h. The analysis uses the Social Relations Model (Back & Kenny, 2010) and dyadic response surfaces (Schönbrodt et al., 2018), with both partners' levels in the model. Each study records who ends each relationship and whom each partner pairs with next, and tests whether gaps on different traits offset or add. For outcomes measured in each partner, the response surface tests the form: curvature along the line of incongruence is the weight, and the peak should sit where the two are level (Edwards & Parry, 1993). For settings, the designs vary task demand while holding peers constant, and peer composition while holding tasks constant. Forced-mixing designs track re-sorting on every axis, and randomly assigned roommates give a further test (Eisenberg et al., 2013).

Leaving is the outcome that counts. Satisfaction shows almost no effect of similarity. Similarity explains under 0.5% of it once each partner's traits are accounted for (Dyrenforth et al., 2010), and response-surface studies find the effect negligible (§3.3, §4). Satisfaction is self-reported, it is dominated by each partner's own dispositions, and it is measured in couples who are still together.

The criterion is refuted if gaps add nothing beyond both sides' levels, if the best match sits well above a person's own level, if those who leave move up and end no closer, or if gaps on separate axes offset each other. The benchmark is person–environment fit research, where fit terms add ΔR² ≈ .008 beyond person and environment effects (Yang et al., 2008). SNT predicts more on the right axes, measured well.

**4. It is distinct.** It sorts on itself, and doesn't reduce to the other axes, to status or to the Big Five. The test is a confirmatory model of the full battery, with every subtest loading on its own axis, fitted against named rivals: a single status factor, and the Big Five. In couple data, an axis's own sorting sits on the diagonal of the cross-partner matrix, and sorting through shared factors sits off it. The same logic gives a ceiling. If partners resemble each other on a trait only through an axis, the trait's partner correlation can't exceed the axis's R² for that trait times the axis's own partner correlation. The criterion is refuted if an axis fails to separate from another axis, from status or from the Big Five, or if its sorting runs through factors shared with other traits.

**Discovery runs two ways.** What the niche demands of a mind nominates candidates, and the test scores them. Data on pairs nominate candidates too: sorting that stays on a single dimension once status and the named axes are removed points to an axis not yet found.

**Measurement follows the IQ test.** Each axis is measured by several different subtests with the axis as their common factor. The subtests are ranked by how little they rest on self-description: objective performance, elicited behaviour, choices, then questionnaires. Their errors come from different sources, which keeps the common factor clean. Axis scores are factor scores, and each subtest reports its loading and its retest reliability. A comparison panel of takers who don't know the theory guards against people performing their self-image. The battery for g, p and c is built and not yet fielded. The h subtests are specified in §3.4 and not yet built.

**Scores are standing within a group.** Where a group shifts the expression of everyone in it, the disposition is standing within that group. c is normed within society and cohort, and h within society and age, because the level of both moves between societies and over time while people hold their ranks (§3.3, §3.4). For p the group is sex (§5.1).

**Power.** Similarity and interaction effects need much larger samples than main effects. Each test is sized by simulating the competing models it separates.

### 2.5 Rivals and relatives

The observation SNT starts from is established. People in every kind of relationship resemble each other (McPherson et al., 2001). Where it has been measured closely, in newlyweds, the resemblance is strong on attitudes and values and weak on personality (Watson et al., 2004; Luo & Klohnen, 2005). SNT adds an account of why, and a test that sorts dimensions by it. The rival taxonomy is the Big Five (§4). Five accounts explain the resemblance without fundamental axes, and each makes a prediction SNT denies.

| Account | It predicts | SNT predicts | Criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Meeting pools** (McPherson et al., 2001) | Sorting vanishes within a pool | It persists there | 1 |
| **Convergence** | Similarity rises with the length of the relationship and is absent from genes | It is there at the start and visible in genes | 2 |
| **Perceived similarity** (Montoya et al., 2008) | Attraction follows the similarity people perceive, and the similarity they have adds nothing | Actual gaps, measured by the battery, end relationships | 3 |
| **Competition** (Becker, 1973; Collado et al., 2023) | All sorting is competition for status, and traits trade against each other | Sorting stays on p, c and h once status is removed, g sorts within a level of status, and gaps add | 3 and 4 |
| **Compatibility is built** (Karney & Bradbury, 1995) | Gaps at the start predict nothing once stress and coping are known | They predict which relationships end | 3 |

**Three traditions share SNT's mechanism and name no dimensions.** Niche picking holds that people select and shape the environments that fit them (Scarr & McCartney, 1983), and that organizations grow homogeneous as the people who fit are attracted, selected and stay (Schneider, 1987). Person–environment fit research measures fit on whatever a study includes, and finds it small. SNT names the few axes on which it should be large. Double empathy holds that a breakdown between differently disposed people runs both ways (Milton, 2012). It is stated for a diagnosis, and SNT states it for any pair, from their gap on p.

**Social niche specialization is a different claim.** In behavioural ecology, the members of a group differentiate into complementary roles, and consistent personalities result (Bergmüller & Taborsky, 2010; Montiglio et al., 2013). There the niche makes the difference. In SNT the difference finds the niche. Both can hold: similarity in relationships kept for their own sake, and differentiation in groups organised around a task.

### 3.1 General intelligence (g)

**Construct.** g is the capacity to learn, reason and handle complexity. It predicts the complexity of the work people do and their attainment across life (Gottfredson, 1997; Strenze, 2007).

**A gap** is felt between people as mismatched pace and depth, and in a setting as boredom or overload. It costs the higher side as well as the lower.

**1. Sorting beyond pools.** Partners correlate .44 on IQ (Horwitz et al., 2023). Preadolescent best friends correlate on tested intelligence after controls (Boutwell et al., 2017), and workers move toward jobs that match their ability (Wilk et al., 1995). Set against the pool, part of the sorting survives. UK couples are more alike on a polygenic score for education (.11) than 97.5% of random re-matches, including re-matches within the same level of education and the same region (Hugh-Jones et al., 2016). In fixed school and university cohorts, the correlation between a student's grades and their friends' grades rose from about .20 to .41 over one to three years. The grades held still and the friendships were rewired (Smirnov & Thurner, 2017). The pool still does much of the work. Half of the sorting on education in Danish couples comes from attending the same or a nearby institution (Nielsen & Svarer, 2009). Among US adolescents, similarity in dating partners' IQ fell from .32 to .18 once the school was held constant (Eastwick et al., 2017).

**2. From fit.** In squadrons built to mix ability, cadets re-sorted by ability, and low-ability cadets chose high-ability study partners less often than chance (Carrell et al., 2013). In speed dating, men's interest in a woman rose with her intelligence up to their own level and stopped there, while women's interest rose throughout (Fisman et al., 2006). The genetic check agrees. In Norwegian couples, partners' polygenic scores for intelligence correlate .04, about what phenotypic sorting predicts (Sunde et al., 2024). Sorting on educational attainment leaves a trace in the genomes of unrelated people: genetic predictors built from odd and even chromosomes correlate (Yengo et al., 2018). Family models of IQ favour sorting on the trait over shared background (Keller et al., 2013).

**3. Gaps end relationships.** The gap in partners' SAT scores predicted breakups among college couples (Hill et al., 1976). Education gaps no longer predict divorce. In recent US cohorts the extra risk for couples in which the wife is more educated has gone (Schwartz & Han, 2014), and it is absent in 458,499 Belgian marriages where such couples are common (Theunis et al., 2018). In settings, placement upward has a cost. The big-fish-little-pond effect on academic self-concept is β = −.28 (Fang et al., 2018), and a 1 SD higher class rank raises later scores by about 0.08 SD (Murphy & Weinhardt, 2020).

**4. Distinct, and distinct from status.** Within sex, g correlates .11–.23 with Realistic and Investigative interests (Pässler et al., 2015), −.20 with c (Onraet et al., 2015) and .02 with h (Anglim et al., 2022). The rival for g's sorting is status.

Status is an outcome. g starts it, and networks and inheritance carry it on. Intelligence measured in youth correlates .56 with later education and .43 with occupational level (Strenze, 2007). Across five cohorts and more than 20,000 people, the sibling with the higher polygenic score for education was the more upwardly mobile (Belsky et al., 2018). Among 4,330 Wisconsin men, each friendship nomination received at school added 2% to wages 35 years later, with IQ in the same model (Conti et al., 2013). Across US counties, the share of friendships that cross class lines correlates .65 with upward mobility (Chetty et al., 2022). Bequests and gifts account for at least half of the parent–child correlation in wealth (Adermon et al., 2018), and a family's standing persists across generations (Clark, 2023).

Status sorts far more strongly than g does. Swedish spouses correlate .75 on latent status, against .49 on schooling (Collado et al., 2023). g explains a quarter to a third of status and sorts at .44. By the ceiling (§2.4), sorting on g alone would leave partners correlated about .11 to .14 on status. Collado et al. find that the rest is mainly non-genetic. Partners' genetic scores for education are more alike than sorting on education predicts, and adjusting for cognitive performance changes that little (Okbay et al., 2022). SNT's reading of the rest is inherited standing and the networks it builds.

g sorts a little within education. In newlyweds, a small similarity in verbal and reasoning ability remained once education and family background were removed (Watkins & Meredith, 1981), and two further samples found the same (Mascie-Taylor & Vandenberg, 1988; Phillips et al., 1988). Phillips et al. found direct sorting on education and on intelligence, and none across the two.

The test puts g before status. Take intelligence measured in adolescence, before attainment, and test whether it predicts the partner's intelligence once the person's own later status is known, and whether status predicts it once intelligence is known. The criterion is refuted for g if intelligence measured before attainment predicts nothing about the partner's intelligence once the person's later status is known.

**Battery.** The battery is randomized matrix problems, forward digit span and symbol-matching speed. Verbal items are left out, so that g doesn't absorb the verbal and social knowledge on which p differs. Matrix items are mechanistic material, so the small correlation of g with things-oriented interests is partly item content.

**Open.** The open questions are whether g sorts within a level of status, whether its genetic trace survives once education is removed, and whether a gap in tested intelligence ends relationships once both partners' levels are known.

### 3.2 People orientation (p)

**Construct.** p is what a mind reads first in anything it meets: how people stand to one another, or how things work. The people-oriented pole tracks standing: who is close to whom, who owes what, who ranks where, and what an act says about the two people involved. It treats talk as something that passes between the speakers. The things-oriented pole tracks workings: causes, parts and rules. It treats talk as the exchange of information. Everyone does both, and p is the balance. The same contrast runs between the relationship and the content of a message (Watzlawick et al., 1967), and between taking part in a relationship and viewing a person from outside it (Strawson, 1962).

Each reading carries across subjects. The things pole reads people as systems to be understood, and the people pole hears a factual correction as a move between two people. Interest in how minds work, as systems, therefore belongs to the things pole, and SNT predicts it loads there.

p drives interests and communication. People choose activities, topics and work by how much they involve people or things. They also bring expectations to any exchange: what belongs in it, what it is for, and how meaning is to be read. SNT predicts that the interest and communication sides load on one factor within each sex.

Five research traditions found p independently: the people–things dimension in vocational psychology (Prediger, 1982; Lippa, 1998; Su et al., 2009), person and thing specialists (Little, 1972), empathizing versus systemizing (Baron-Cohen, 2002; Greenberg et al., 2018), mechanistic versus mentalistic cognition (Crespi & Badcock, 2008), and opposed social and physical reasoning networks in the brain (Jack et al., 2013). Each measured something narrower than p, and SNT predicts their measures share one factor.

p is an allocation, which is why it behaves as one axis. In absolute ratings, interest in people and interest in things are separate. Opposite interest types correlate only −.06 to −.23 once general interest is removed (Tay et al., 2011). Time and attention spent on one is not spent on the other, and in relative measures (profile shape, choices, shares of time or gaze) one bipolar dimension dominates within each sex (Lippa, 2005). Niches are chosen by allocation, so the relative measure is the one that sorts.

p is an interest. The sex difference in people–things interest is d = .93 (Su et al., 2009). In reading mental states from the eyes it is d = .21, across about 305,000 people (Greenberg et al., 2023). People differ far more in where they put their attention than in what they can perceive.

**A gap** comes from both sides. Two people at a gap want to do and talk about different things, and they misread each other when they talk. A correction offered as useful information is heard as a claim of superiority. Reassurance offered as care is heard as evasion. Neither involves low intelligence or bad intentions.

**1. Sorting beyond pools.** Young children choose playmates by similarity in gender-typed activities, beyond the playmate's sex (Martin et al., 2013). Occupation explains 20% of the variance in mechanical interests (Nye, Perlus & Rounds, 2018). Partners are similar on Realistic, Investigative, Artistic and Social interests beyond the Big Five (Banov et al., 2023, 215 couples). Spouses' interest profiles correlate +.18, against −.18 for random pairs from the same sample (Etzel et al., 2019).

The pool does most of the work for field of study. In Norway, couples share a field at .165, against .038 for random pairs, and admission cutoffs show that enrolling is what produces the match (Kirkebøen et al., 2021). Dutch applicants who lost an admission lottery for their chosen study were only marginally more likely than chance to marry someone from it (Artmann et al., 2021). Choosing a field is sorting into a setting, and counts for p when the choice was made on p. Field and occupation are therefore evidence of sorting into settings, and weak evidence of sorting between partners.

**2. From fit.** The evidence is the friction itself, seen where p is most extreme: autistic people sit at its things-oriented end (§5.3). Blind observers rate rapport lowest in pairs of one autistic and one non-autistic person, and at least as high in autistic pairs as in non-autistic pairs (Crompton et al., 2020b). Later studies find the mixed-pair cost again (Rifai et al., 2022; Foster et al., 2025). Information passes equally well along matched and mixed chains (Crompton et al., 2025), so the cost of a gap falls on rapport and leaves comprehension intact. The matched advantage is uneven: in self-ratings non-autistic pairs come out best, and part of the mixed-pair cost is stigma. The diagnosis also mixes p with impairment (§5.3).

**3. Gaps end relationships.** Among non-autistic friends, the gap in autistic traits predicts lower friendship quality (r = −.40) and shorter friendships (r = −.37), better than either friend's own score (Bolis et al., 2021). In settings, interest fit predicts persistence (.36) and performance (.30 to .32) in work and study (Nye et al., 2012, 2017). Students who drop out have poorer interest fit, while their satisfaction barely tracks it (Ertl et al., 2022).

**4. Distinct.** Within sex, g correlates only .11–.23 with Realistic and Investigative interests (Pässler et al., 2015). In 656 Dutch participants, people orientation correlated .40 with Emotionality, .24 with Extraversion and .18 with Honesty–Humility. These figures pool the sexes, and sex correlated .59 with people orientation, so the structure that matters is within sex (Holtrop et al., 2015). Realistic interests are unrelated to any Big Five trait (multiple R = .11; Barrick et al., 2003). On content-free measures p and c are close to independent: vocational interests show no link to conformity and tradition values (Sagiv, 2002). The criterion is refuted for p if p fails to separate from g and the Big Five, if p and c correlate above .5, or if interest in how minds work, as systems, loads on the people pole.

**Roots.** Women exposed to high prenatal androgens through congenital adrenal hyperplasia are more things-oriented than unaffected women (d = 0.75), in proportion to their virilization (Beltz et al., 2011). Male rhesus monkeys prefer wheeled toys to plush ones, as boys do (Hassett et al., 2008). Newborn female macaques look at faces more than males do, before social experience can explain it (Simpson et al., 2016). Social role theory traces the human difference to the division of labour itself (Eagly & Wood, 1999). The hormonal and primate evidence points to a disposition that the division of labour then amplified.

**Battery.** The battery has three parts. The first is the eight-item people/things questionnaire (Graziano et al., 2012), with forced-choice items. The second is viewing shares on social versus mechanical images, using many images and scored with a hierarchical model. Free-viewing designs of this kind reach retest reliability of .7–.9 and are heritable (de Haas et al., 2019; Constantino et al., 2017). The third is elicited messages for coordination, refusal and repair, scored with established text features, together with choices among truthful communication strategies. p is scored net of g and normed within sex (§5.1).

**Open.** Its evidence of sorting between partners is the thinnest of the four, because couples who share a field of study mostly met there. No study has set measured interests against a known pool, separated fit from convergence in couples, or tested who leaves.

### 3.3 Conventionalism (c)

**Construct.** c is the authority a person grants to consensus: the weight they give to what their group does and has done, against their own judgment, in deciding what is true and right. At the conventional pole, a norm's existence is evidence of its worth and a claim on oneself. At the autonomous pole, a norm is a hypothesis, binding only once one has judged it.

c has two faces (Schwartz, 1992): tradition, deference to the group's past norms, and conformity, deference to its present ones. Where norms change quickly the two come apart, so the battery measures both.

c is a disposition, and it attaches to whatever order a person belongs to. In the late Soviet Union, people high on right-wing authoritarianism supported communism, the established authority there (McFarland et al., 1992). Across 51 nations, valuing conformity, security and tradition predicts right-wing cultural attitudes, but on average left-wing economic attitudes (Malka et al., 2014). In Singapore, where the state promotes multiculturalism, authoritarianism goes with more liking of out-groups, the reverse of Belgium (Roets et al., 2015). Religious conservatism is c's content where the inherited order is religious.

Six research traditions measure parts of c: conformist versus individual learning in cultural evolution (Boyd & Richerson, 1985), Traditionalism (Tellegen & Waller, 2008), the conventionalism facet of right-wing authoritarianism (Altemeyer, 1981; Duckitt et al., 2010), conformity versus autonomy in child-rearing values (Feldman & Stenner, 1997), the conservation values (Schwartz, 1992), and the values facet of Openness at its low pole (McCrae et al., 2008).

**A gap** is a disagreement about what counts as a reason, and each side reads the other as disloyal or as arbitrary. In a setting it is felt as stifling order or as lawlessness.

**1. Sorting beyond pools.** Spouses correlate .65 on conservatism and .71 on church attendance (Alford et al., 2011, about 5,000 couples). Conservatism and attendance are content, which people also sort on directly (§5.2). On the personality scale itself, spouses correlate .49 on Traditionalism, against a median of about .1 on the other scales of the same inventory (Humbad et al., 2010, 1,296 couples). Traditionalism is the closest existing measure of the disposition. Across 18 million couples in US voter files, 9% to 10% pair a Democrat with a Republican, whichever way the neighbourhood leans (Hersh & Ghitza, 2018).

**2. From fit.** Spousal similarity on Traditionalism doesn't grow with years of marriage (Humbad et al., 2010). Controlling both partners' family background lowers the spousal correlation on liberal–conservative attitudes only from .647 to .628, and couples married a year or less already correlate .59 (Alford et al., 2011). Partners similar on authoritarianism don't converge over time (Miculka & Imhoff, 2026).

**3. Gaps end relationships.** Gaps in content predict breakups. Catholic–Protestant couples dissolved at 18.4% over ten years, against 11.4% for same-faith couples (adjusted OR 1.47; Wright et al., 2017, 19,791 couples). Couples who back different parties separate 38% more often (Arpino & Di Nallo, 2025). Two equally conventional people of different faiths have no gap on c, so these are gaps in content (§5.2). Gaps nearer the disposition are mixed. Gaps in attendance predict divorce only when the husband attends more (Vaaler et al., 2009), or not at all (Hwang et al., 2019). Similarity on Openness, where c sits (§4), predicted which couples stayed together in two panels (Rammstedt et al., 2013; Hofmann & Krapf, 2024). Satisfaction shows nothing: couples correlate .47 to .57 on authoritarianism, and the gap between them doesn't predict satisfaction (Miculka & Imhoff, 2026).

**4. Distinct.** c is distinct from g. It correlates −.20 with cognitive ability (Onraet et al., 2015), and within families, polygenic scores for cognition predict lower authoritarianism (Edwards et al., 2024). The sorting tests therefore use c net of g. Reasoning about norms is g. Granting one's own judgment authority over them is c. c is distinct from p and h as well: vocational interests show no link to conformity and tradition values (Sagiv, 2002), and the conventionalism facet correlates −.01 with Honesty–Humility (de Vries et al., 2022).

Three neighbouring dispositions are separate from c. Authoritarian aggression, submission and censorship appear on the left as well as the right (Costello et al., 2022), and what belongs to c is deference to the established order. Legalism is following an explicit rule because it is the rule, even against the group. c defers to what the group does, and legalism to what the rule says. The two pull apart on p: autistic children conform less to a group's wrong answers (Yafai et al., 2014), yet autistic adults punish norm violations as much as others do, and favour their own group less when they punish (Qian et al., 2022). Rebellion is separate too, since an autonomous person can judge a tradition and keep it. The criterion is refuted for c if c fails to separate from g and the Big Five, or if consensus-weight and legalism relate to p with the same sign.

**Roots.** Right-wing authoritarianism is about 50% heritable in twins reared together and apart (McCourt et al., 1999). Authoritarianism, conservatism and religiousness share one genetic factor with Traditionalism, with heritability .44 (Ludeke et al., 2013).

**Norming.** What the conventional view is has changed greatly within a century, so a scale with fixed content measures different things in different cohorts. The content moves. Over 54 years, the traits American mothers wanted in their children shifted from obedience to autonomy (Alwin, 1988), and those traits are the items of the child-rearing measure. Conformity in the Asch line-judgment task fell across US studies with the date of the study (Bond & Smith, 1996). The ranks hold. Authoritarianism correlates .74 with itself across 15 or more years within one cohort (Ludeke & Krueger, 2013). From 1992 to 2012, Americans high in authoritarianism grew less negative toward sexual minorities as the norm changed (Oyamot et al., 2017).

**Battery.** The battery has five parts. Copying in social-learning tasks measures how far people follow the majority against their own information, which differs consistently between individuals (Molleman et al., 2014; Toelch et al., 2014). Elicited justifications record whether a person, asked why a rule should be kept, cites precedent and authority or reasons and consequences. The four child-rearing choices (Feldman & Stenner, 1997) carry no political content. Consensus-versus-rule choices are ones in which the group's behaviour, an explicit rule and the person's own evidence point different ways, built on the rule-following task of Kimbrough & Vostroknutov (2016). Weight on the group is c. Weight on the rule is legalism, scored separately. The fifth part is third-party sanctioning of norm violations (Fehr & Fischbacher, 2004), with records of practice such as attendance and the timing of marriage and first birth. c is scored net of g and of Agreeableness, so that capacity and conformity for the sake of warmth drop out, and within the person's society and cohort.

**Open.** The open questions are whether c's content-free core carries the sorting, whether that sorting survives a known pool, and whether a gap on the disposition ends relationships once content is held constant (§5.2).

### 3.4 Honesty (h)

**Construct.** h is the weight a mind gives to others' interests against its own. Honesty is what that weight looks like in behaviour, and it gives the axis its name: a person who counts others' interests doesn't deceive or exploit them when it would pay. The same weight shows in fairness, giving at a cost to oneself and being trustworthy, and at the low end in cheating and exploitation.

h is one dimension. Giving in dictator, public-goods and trust games correlates .41 on average from one game to another, and the decisions load on one factor (Peysakhovich et al., 2014, N = 576). The factor common to psychopathy, narcissism and the other "dark" traits correlates −.80 with Honesty–Humility (Moshagen et al., 2018). Honesty–Humility, the sincerity, fairness and greed-avoidance scale of Ashton and Lee (2007), predicts prosocial play in economic games (ρ = .20, k = 80; Thielmann et al., 2020) and less cheating (OR = 0.53, N = 5,002; Heck et al., 2018).

Four things are left out of h. Trusting others is a belief. It correlates about .1 with a person's own honesty, and it rises with intelligence (Thielmann et al., 2020; Carl & Billari, 2014). Being trustworthy belongs to h. Punishing others loads on a separate factor (Peysakhovich et al., 2014). Not retaliating is the rest of Agreeableness (Hilbig et al., 2013). Rule-breaking and crime go more with impulsivity and substance use. Antisocial behaviour correlates .65 to .69 genetically with a factor of substance use and risk taking (Karlsson Linnér et al., 2021). Crime is also related to g: Swedish conscripts later convicted of violence averaged 3.8 on a nine-point ability scale against 5.2 for the rest, and about half of that link held between brothers (Frisell et al., 2012b, 700,514 men).

**A gap** is a disagreement about what each owes the other, and it costs both sides. The more honest side gives more than they get. The less honest side expects to be cheated: people's expectations of how trustworthy others are follow how trustworthy they are themselves (Thielmann & Hilbig, 2014).

**1. Sorting beyond pools.** Spouses correlate .39 on Honesty–Humility in 451 long-married Dutch couples (Lee et al., 2026), close to the .44 for g and the .49 for c, and .23 in a German sample of twins' spouses (Kandler et al., 2019). Senegalese spouses are alike in what they give in a public-goods game (Tognetti et al., 2014). Partners are alike on the dark traits by a small to medium amount (Richards et al., 2024).

Friends correlate about .25 on Honesty–Humility (Lee et al., 2009). Among Hadza foragers, people who give more in a public-goods game have campmates and gift partners who give more (Apicella et al., 2012). People also sort into work. Danish students who want careers in public service cheat less in a dice task, and Indian students who want government jobs cheat more, each in the direction of the sector's own record (Barfort et al., 2019; Hanna & Wang, 2017). Among US financial advisers with a record of misconduct, about half lose their job, and 44% of those are rehired within a year, by firms with more misconduct of their own (Egan et al., 2019).

Wider measures mix h with other things. Spouses correlate .50 on trust (Dohmen et al., 2012, 6,873 couples), .41 on liability to violent crime in Swedish registers (Frisell et al., 2012a), and .53 on antisocial behaviour (Krueger et al., 1998).

**2. From fit.** Sorting and convergence both appear. In the one spouses-of-twins study, of about 100 pairs, similarity on Honesty–Humility came from shared background and convergence, while Openness, in the same couples, was sorted on directly (Kandler et al., 2019). Trust rises from .34 in newlyweds to .50 in all couples (Dohmen et al., 2012), and Dutch couples' offending is alike before marriage and more alike after (van de Weijer & Beaver, 2017). Among 544 Dutch pupils, friends were chosen for similar delinquency and did not make each other more alike (Knecht et al., 2010). Another network study found the reverse (Weerman, 2011). The level follows the company. Among the Hadza, a person's giving was unstable from year to year and tracked the giving of their current camp (Smith et al., 2018). Convergence alone fails this criterion. SNT reads convergence inside a relationship as the gap closing, since people match the cooperation they receive.

**3. Gaps end relationships.** The honest side leaves. Where people can drop partners in a cooperation game, cooperators drop defectors, and pairs of cooperators last longest (Rand et al., 2011, 785 participants). In Danish registers, a man's conviction raises the risk that his union ends by about 76% (Svarer, 2011). Levels act as well (§2.2). Dutch offenders divorce more often when the spouse offends too (van Schellen, 2012). A pair matched at the low end is therefore less stable than a pair matched at the high end. SNT predicts that a pair matched at the low end still outlasts a pair of one low and one high, once each side's own level is counted.

Drinking shows the pattern, on a different trait. In 19,977 Norwegian couples, the hazard of divorce was 3.07 when only the wife drank heavily, 1.51 when only the husband did, and 1.63 when both did, far below what the two separate effects would give (Torvik et al., 2013). Matched pairs at the low end hold together where it pays. In a game that paid pairs for matching reports, liars sought out liars, and pairs in which both lied asked to change partner 3.5% of the time (Gross et al., 2018).

One lab result runs against the prediction. Pairs of two defectors were less stable than mixed pairs (Rand et al., 2011). The choices there are paid and real, so they are behaviour. The pairings last minutes, between strangers, and the partner's cooperation is the only thing on offer, so two defectors have nothing to stay for. The test that counts is the ending of real relationships, with both sides' h in the model.

**4. Distinct.** Honesty–Humility correlates .02 with intelligence (N = 32,165; Anglim et al., 2022), about .06 with people–things orientation within sex (Holtrop et al., 2015), and −.01 with the conventionalism facet of authoritarianism (de Vries et al., 2022, 7,067 twins and siblings). g can't account for the sorting. At .02, the ceiling (§2.4) leaves sorting on g nothing to contribute. Sorting on trust falls only from .50 to .43 with both partners' schooling, income, religion and region held constant (Dohmen et al., 2012, 6,320 couples). For crime, the ceiling gives g at most .05 of the .41. h overlaps with impulsivity: Honesty–Humility correlates −.38 with impulsivity (Moshagen et al., 2018). The criterion is refuted for h if h fails to separate from impulsivity, or if its sorting runs through status.

**Roots.** Honesty–Humility is 30–46% heritable (Kandler et al., 2019), giving in the dictator game 22% to 31% (Cesarini et al., 2009), and antisocial behaviour 41% across 51 twin and adoption studies (Rhee & Waldman, 2002).

**Norming.** The level moves between societies and with age, and sex moves it little. In a die-rolling task given to 2,568 students in 23 countries, the share who reported honestly ran from 4% to 87%, and fell with the country's level of corruption and tax evasion (Gächter & Schulz, 2016). Across 565 experiments, lying falls by about a quarter of a point with each year of age, and 42% of men lied against 38% of women (Gerlach et al., 2019).

**Battery.** The battery uses giving in dictator and public-goods games and returning in the trust game, each played several times, because one game correlates only about .4 with itself four months later (Peysakhovich et al., 2014). It adds choices between payoffs for oneself and for another person, which hold their rank order at .64 over two years (Frei et al., 2025). It also uses repeated cheating tasks, because a single die roll can't score a person (Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi, 2013), and the Honesty–Humility questionnaire (Ashton & Lee, 2007). Trusting and punishing are scored separately. h is scored apart from impulsivity, and within the person's society and age band. Lab measures carry into the field: cheating in the lab goes with fare-dodging and with fraudulent absence from work (Dai et al., 2018; Hanna & Wang, 2017).

**Open.** The open questions are whether h's sorting survives a known pool, whether the less honest side also leaves a mixed pair, whether a pair matched at the low end outlasts a mixed pair, how much of the resemblance is built inside the relationship, and where h ends and impulsivity begins.

## 4. What the Big Five Blend

The Big Five are the standard account of how people differ, and SNT is a rival to it. Neuroticism is the exception, and SNT keeps it (§4.5).

### 4.1 Built from words, measured by self-description

The method was description. The five factors came from factor analysis of the adjectives people use for each other (Goldberg, 1990). That method finds how people are judged. Nothing in it asks whether a factor predicts anything, and no theory says why there should be five (Block, 1995). The structure doesn't travel. In face-to-face surveys of 94,751 people in 23 low- and middle-income countries, the standard questions generally failed to measure the intended traits (Laajaj et al., 2019). The five factors are a poor summary of their own items. Across 53 outcomes, single questionnaire items predicted better than the five domains, and they kept their predictive power after the domains were removed (Stewart et al., 2022).

A self-rating is a comparison with the people around oneself. People rate themselves against their own group, so scores from different groups can't be compared (Heine et al., 2002). SNT makes that comparison explicit and norms it (§2.4). Other people's ratings predict better than one's own (Connelly & Ones, 2010). Self-report hides sorting. Measured from Facebook Likes and from language, partner similarity on the five traits averages .24 and .36, against .09 on questionnaires in the same data (Youyou et al., 2017). Likes and words also carry shared culture, so these are upper bounds.

### 4.2 They predict weakly

The effects of the Big Five are real and small. In preregistered replications of 78 published links between traits and life outcomes, 87% replicated, at 77% of the original size (Soto, 2019). Much of what they predict is self-report predicting self-report. In the 53-outcome study, traits and outcomes were questionnaires answered by the same person (Stewart et al., 2022).

The yardstick here is attainment. Education, occupational level and income are taken from records, cover everyone, and show where a person ended up. In 81,075 Americans followed for eleven years from high school, no Big Five trait correlated above .16 with education or occupational prestige, against .52 and .45 for intelligence (Damian et al., 2015). Occupation barely separates people on the Big Five. It explains 2–7% of the variance in Big Five traits (Anni et al., 2025), against 20% for mechanical interests (Nye, Perlus & Rounds, 2018).

As measured, the Big Five say little about relationships. Partners correlate .1 to .2 on most of them. None of 80 actor-by-partner tests of similarity predicted divorce (Solomon & Jackson, 2014). Response-surface studies find the effect of Big Five similarity on satisfaction negligible in 237, 1,294 and 4,464 couples (Weidmann et al., 2017, 2023; van Scheppingen et al., 2019).

### 4.3 Trait by trait

Three of the five traits blend things that sorting separates, so the charge is against the unit as well as the instrument.

The sorting sits in the Values facet (.23–.39), and the intellect facet sorts at .09–.16 (McCrae et al., 2008), while tested intelligence sorts at .44. **For kindness, better measurement adds a little.** Other people agree with a person's own rating of Agreeableness less than on any other Big Five trait, by a small margin: .29, against .34 to .41 (Connelly & Ones, 2010). Combining self-ratings with spouse ratings raises partner similarity on it to between .16 and .34 (McCrae et al., 2008). The larger gain comes from splitting the trait.

**c's lead over Openness comes partly from scales that share content with what they predict.** Values explained 17% to 24% of the vote in Italy, against 9% for traits, and traits added nothing beyond values (Caprara et al., 2006). With the content removed the link shrinks. How far a person moves toward someone else's estimate in a judgment task predicts authoritarianism at b = 0.16, and attitudes to religious instruction and abortion. Following an arbitrary rule predicts none of it (ρ = .02; Claessens et al., 2023), which fits the split between c and legalism (§3.3). The child-rearing items also follow a person's politics over time as much as they predict it (Bakker et al., 2021).

**Interests against the Big Five.** The lead of interests holds within each sex: Realistic and Social interests separate fields of study about equally well among women and among men (R² = .65 and .64; Pässler & Hell, 2012). Sex carries much of the pooled effect. With sex and family background entered first, the variance in income that interests add falls from .16 to .02 (Su, 2012). Students in different majors differ on all five traits, often by medium amounts (Vedel, 2016), and Extraversion and Agreeableness overlap with interest profiles more than was thought (Etzel et al., 2025).

**Sociability is partly a state of fit.** Within sex, people and things interests correlate under .10 with sociability and sympathy in Project Talent (Su, 2012), so p is largely separate from Extraversion. SNT predicts that people are much less sociable and less warm among people at a gap from them, and that a questionnaire answered from inside a mismatched setting records the mismatch as low Extraversion and low Agreeableness. Autistic adults disclosed more to autistic partners than to non-autistic ones (Morrison et al., 2020), and pairs of two extraverts or two introverts had better first conversations than mixed pairs (Cuperman & Ickes, 2009). The test is sociable behaviour recorded in the same people across matched and mismatched company. The prediction is refuted if that behaviour is the same in both.

### 4.4 The head-to-head test

Give both partners in a sample of couples the battery and a Big Five inventory, and compare two models on held-out data, one on the axes and one on the Big Five. The first comparison is about the person: each partner's education, field, occupation, income, and political and religious practice, predicted from their own levels. The second is about the relationship: which pairings are real when set among shuffled ones, and which couples break up, predicted from both partners' levels and their gaps.

SNT predicts the axes win on both, and that adding the Big Five to the axes improves prediction less than adding the axes to the Big Five. SNT is refuted if the Big Five predict where a person ends up, or pairing and breakup, as well as the axes do.

### 4.5 Where the Big Five hold up

Conscientiousness predicts grades about as well as intelligence does (Poropat, 2009). The Big Five predicted health better than interests did (Stoll et al., 2017). SNT's answer to these two is the head-to-head test and the test of fit against wellbeing and health (§5.3). If the Big Five match the axes there, SNT loses.

**Neuroticism is a stable and strongly predictive trait, and SNT keeps it.** People with anxiety, depressive and substance use disorders score far above others on it (mean d = 1.65; Kotov et al., 2010). It predicts later anxiety and depression at d = 0.50–0.70. After adjustment for baseline symptoms and psychiatric history the link is d = 0.10–0.40, and it barely decays with time (Jeronimus et al., 2016). It predicts mental and physical disorders and the use of health services, beyond what overlap between the measures explains (Lahey, 2009).

Neuroticism meets the definition of a fundamental dimension: a person's standing on it predicts how their life differs. It lies outside SNT's test, which finds the dimensions that enter relationships. Anxiety is carried by the person who has it, and a gap on it leaves neither side short. Partners barely sort on it: .04 in UK Biobank couples and .11 across studies (Horwitz et al., 2023). The sex difference can't hide a match, because a correlation ignores each sex's average. Every test of fit keeps neuroticism in the model and asks what fit adds.

## 5. Three Side Hypotheses

### 5.1 Matching within sex on p

The sexes differ by d = .93 on people–things interest, one of the largest differences in psychology (Su et al., 2009), and they evolved different specializations (Trivers, 1972). Yet humans choose mates mutually (Stewart-Williams & Thomas, 2013), and within each sex people vary widely on the same axis.

**The percentile matching rule.** The same variation shifts both sexes along the axis, and sex sets the baseline. A man and a woman at the same percentile of their own sex share the same disposition, though their raw scores differ, and friction between them depends on that shared disposition.

**The rival is matching on raw scores.** Friction as §3.2 describes it runs on expressed behaviour: what two people want to do, and how each reads the other. That predicts that partners match on raw scores. The percentile rule holds only if each person reads the other against the baseline of the other's sex, so that the same remark means one thing from a man and another from a woman. The partner correlation can't reveal the rule, because standardizing within sex leaves it unchanged.

**The test compares partnered with single adults,** within each sex and age band, with p measured directly. Under raw-distance matching, sex-atypical people find nearer partners, while sex-typical people in the tails find none. Partnered men then lean toward the people pole and partnered women toward the things pole. Under percentile matching, no such shift appears.

Confounds that make people more likely to partner push both sexes the same way, so the opposite-sign component isolates the matching rule. The method is calibrated on height first. Height is measured almost without error, has a large sex difference, and has been compared with randomized pairings in 12,502 UK couples (Stulp et al., 2013). Same-sex couples, for whom the two rules coincide, give the reference strength of sorting.

The same logic applies to the other groups. Under rank matching, an age-gap couple is more similar on cohort-normed c than on raw c, and a couple from two societies is more similar on society-normed h than on raw h. The percentile rule is refuted if partnered and single people differ on p in opposite directions by sex.

### 5.2 The cultural layer

Sorting has two layers. The deep layer is drift on the fundamental axes. The cultural layer is the content through which the axes are expressed, such as party and denomination. c sets how tightly a person binds to that content: conventional people hold the content of their milieu as binding, and autonomous people hold it as provisional. Which party or faith expresses c depends on the society, and people also sort on the identity directly.

In online dating, people disproportionately contact others who share their politics (Huber & Malhotra, 2017). That is direct sorting on a signalled identity. Couples who disagreed on Brexit separated at 1.8% a year, against 1.1% for couples who agreed (Arpino & Di Nallo, 2025). Under terrorism threat, people low in authoritarianism move toward authoritarian positions, while those high in it stay where they are (Hetherington & Suhay, 2011). Threat narrows gaps on c while it lasts.

The ceiling (§2.4) applies. Current personality and cognitive measures explain about 15–20% of ideology. A content-free measure of c shows how much of the .58 political correlation between spouses belongs to the deep layer.

SNT makes three predictions. The first is that content attaches to the milieu: measured with content-free subtests, c predicts sanctioning of violations of the norms of a person's own milieu, religious norms in a religious community and progressive norms in a progressive institution. The second is that content gaps cost more at high c: gaps in faith and party predict breakups more strongly when both partners are high on c. The third is that the cost of a gap follows salience and threat: it rises when the dimension is salient and falls under shared threat.

Two designs test them. Give the c battery to people in milieus whose norms differ, and measure third-party sanctioning of violations of each milieu's norms. In household panels, interact partners' gaps on c and on content with each other, and with periods of salience and threat, with both partners' levels in the model. The hypothesis is refuted if sorting on deep dispositions doesn't concentrate in a few dimensions, or if c predicts the same political content in every milieu, which would make it an ideology.

### 5.3 Misfit and deficit

A person at the far end of an axis, in a setting built for the other end, meets friction in every exchange. From outside, that looks like a deficit in the person. A diagnosis that defines a problem by behaviour and locates it in the person can't tell misfit from impairment.

**People are better off in their niche.** SNT predicts that fit with one's company and settings raises wellbeing and health. There is an animal precedent. Across 35 species of zoo carnivore, the wider a species ranges in the wild, the more it paces in captivity and the more of its infants die (Clubb & Mason, 2003). The animal is sound and the setting is wrong for it.

Fit with a community shows in clinical records. Among members of ethnic minorities, each 10-point drop in the local share of their own group raises the odds of psychosis by 1.20 (Baker et al., 2021). Denmark assigned 44,033 refugees to housing, and those placed among the fewest co-nationals had 1.25 times the rate of psychosis and 1.21 times the rate of post-traumatic stress disorder (Schofield et al., 2024). Assignment rules out people choosing where they fit. A disposition also pays where it is the norm. Across 2.2 million people in 65 countries, religiosity predicts self-esteem with a slope of .20 in the most religious country and .04 in the least (Gebauer et al., 2017). Religious people lived 2.2 years longer in religious US counties and had no advantage in the others (Ebert et al., 2020).

On questionnaire personality, fit adds almost nothing. Across 543,934 people in 860 US cities, the fit between a person and their city raised the explained variance in self-esteem by at most 0.11 percentage points, where the person's own traits explained 5% to 27% (Bleidorn et al., 2016). Some results run the other way. Among 2 million Danes, growing up poorer than one's neighbourhood did not raise the risk of psychosis (Schofield et al., 2023). Fit with one's country on the values of self-direction and stimulation went with lower wellbeing (Hanel et al., 2020). The effect of a group's local share is confined to visible minorities, so discrimination is a rival explanation.

The test gives the battery to a person and to their household, classroom or workplace, and scores fit as distance. The outcomes come from clinical records, sick leave and wellbeing measures, with both sides' levels and the Big Five, neuroticism above all, in the model. Changes of school, job or team give within-person tests, and random assignment of rooms and classes gives causal ones. The hypothesis is refuted if fit adds nothing to wellbeing and health beyond the person's own traits, or if people who move to a better-fitting setting don't improve.

**Autism is the worked case.** The diagnosis mixes two sources of social difficulty that look alike from outside. The first is position: the things-oriented tail of p. It goes with average or high g and with common, inherited genetic variants. The difficulty is friction: a mechanistic communicator in a people-oriented setting is misread, and misreads others, every day. The second is impairment. It goes with lower g, often intellectual disability, and with rare or new (de novo) damaging mutations. The difficulty comes from the brain and follows the person into every setting. The two can occur together. Common and rare variants add (Weiner et al., 2017), so a diagnosed person can carry either or both.

**The criteria can't tell the two apart.** DSM-5 defines autism by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction "across multiple contexts", together with restricted and repetitive behaviour (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). None of the criteria asks whether the difficulty fades when the person is among others like them. SNT predicts that friction fades there and impairment doesn't.

Four lines of evidence point to two sources: genetics, cognition, rapport and the history of the diagnosis. Common variants that raise autism risk are positively genetically correlated with educational attainment (Grove et al., 2019). Rare de novo damaging variants concentrate in autistic people with intellectual disability (Weiner et al., 2017). Autistic people score far higher on Raven's matrices, a nonverbal reasoning test, than on Wechsler scales, which weigh verbal and social knowledge (Dawson et al., 2007). Autistic pairs show rapport at least as high as non-autistic pairs, and mixed pairs the lowest (§3.2). Prevalence has risen from about 4 in 10,000 in the 1970s to 1 in 31 American eight-year-olds (Shaw et al., 2025), and over the same period the diagnosis absorbed Asperger's syndrome. As the net widened, it took in more of the things-oriented tail. Clinicians have called for a return to narrower prototypes (Mottron, 2021).

SNT predicts that, within diagnosed samples, the group with high g and low rare-variant burden shows normal rapport with matched partners, and difficulty that tracks the p of their setting. The other group shows difficulty in every setting. The test, in diagnosed adults, measures g, rare-variant burden, polygenic scores, and p with the battery. It then measures rapport and social functioning with matched and unmatched partners, and in settings that differ in p. The hypothesis is refuted if social difficulty in the high-g, low-rare-variant group is as independent of setting and partner as in the other group.

**ADHD is the next case.** Its symptoms are defined against a setting: sitting still, holding attention on assigned work, waiting. Across 25 studies and 8 million children, the youngest in a class are 1.34 times as likely to be diagnosed (Caye et al., 2020). In Denmark, where many of the youngest start school a year late, the ratio for medication falls to 1.08 (Pottegård et al., 2014). Children with ADHD are more active than others by d = 1.39 under high executive demand and by d = 0.36 under low cognitive load (Kofler et al., 2016). A large part is impairment: ADHD is about 74% heritable (Faraone & Larsson, 2019), and it doubles mortality, mainly through accidents (Dalsgaard et al., 2015). In one long follow-up, periods of remission went with higher demands on the person, which runs against the claim (Sibley et al., 2024). The claim is refuted if symptoms and impairment stay the same across settings that differ in what they demand.

The strongest evidence is attainment taken from records, where intelligence and interests outpredict the Big Five in the same people; same-sample comparisons of conventionalism with Openness; and sorting that survives comparison with random pairs from the same pool. The weakest is sorting on p, once the pool is set aside; g's sorting within a level of education, which is small in the three samples that tested it; the lead of c over Openness once political content is removed from its scales; and fit as a predictor of wellbeing, which shows in records for minority status and religiosity and is close to nothing for questionnaire personality.

Five results run against the theory. Second marriages are less alike on education than first ones, where the theory expects people to settle closer. Response-surface studies find no effect of partners' similarity on satisfaction, on authoritarianism included. Trades between traits are weak and present. In a partner-choice experiment, pairs of two defectors were less stable than mixed pairs. One long follow-up found ADHD in remission during periods of higher demand. Every criterion as stated is untested on g, p, c and h measured by the battery.

Four studies decide most of this. The first is an entering-cohort study. Give the battery to everyone entering a college or a firm, assign rooms and work groups at random, and record who befriends whom, who re-sorts, and who leaves. It tests criteria 1, 2 and 3 outside marriage, with the meeting pool known. Each person's wellbeing and health, set against their fit, tests §5.3. The second is a newlywed couples study, with the battery given to both partners and the couples followed for breakups, who ends them, and whom each partner pairs with next. It tests all four criteria on all four axes at once, and sets the axes against the Big Five on the same couples (§4.4). The third is the partnered-versus-single comparison on p, which tests the matching rule between the sexes (§5.1). The fourth is a stratified study of autistic adults, which tests whether part of the diagnosis is a problem of fit (§5.3).

Human minds differ on a few fundamental dimensions, and the social world finds them. People move toward the company and settings that fit them, and that leaves them sorted on the dimensions that matter. g, p, c and h are the candidates so far, each a basic way in which minds differ. The list is open. Whatever passes the test joins it, and what survives is the short list of dimensions on which human minds differ in ways that decide where people thrive and whose company they keep.
