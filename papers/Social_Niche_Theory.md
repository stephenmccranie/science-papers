# Social Niche Theory: A Framework for Niche Fit and Mismatch

**Author:** Stephen McCranie

**Date:** September 2026

**Status:** Pre-publication hypothesis paper

---

## Abstract

People differ in the environments and relationships in which they thrive. Social Niche Theory (SNT) proposes three dimensions of social sorting: **general intelligence (g)**, **cognitive orientation (o)**, and **cooperativeness (c)**. These describe the complexity people can handle, the impersonal-mechanistic or social-relational terms on which they interpret and communicate, and their willingness to cooperate honestly and reciprocally. SNT predicts that this combination organizes partner selection, peer affiliation, and environmental fit.

Observed partner assortment provides the empirical starting point. The theory proposes that reliable assortment across broader traits partly reflects their relationships to g, o, and c, and predicts that between-sex partners resemble each other in standing within their own sex rather than in raw levels—a matching-rule hypothesis that remains to be tested against explicit alternatives. Educational grouping and acceleration research supplies complementary evidence that changing placement can improve objective performance. The neural hypothesis links intelligence and orientation to shared cognitive capacity and the relative engagement of task-positive and default-mode processing.

An implemented pilot battery combines cognitive performance, visual engagement, elicited communication, and questionnaires. Its purpose is to measure the proposed dimensions with limited dependence on self-description. The central tests are whether these measures identify a useful three-dimensional structure, explain observed sorting beyond existing markers, and improve outcomes when used for placement.

**Keywords:** social niche, general intelligence, cognitive orientation, cooperativeness, assortative mating, person–environment fit, psychometrics, DMN, TPN

---

## 1. The Problem

The same person can be effective and comfortable in one setting yet disengaged, exhausted, or isolated in another. People also differ in whom they can think with, what they enjoy discussing, and what they expect from cooperation. SNT asks whether a compact description of these differences can improve how people find partners, peers, and places to work and learn.

The proposed description has three dimensions: intelligence, orientation, and cooperativeness. A social niche fits their combination. The practical aim is to measure the person and identify settings in which thought and cooperation come more easily.

Person–environment fit research provides the closest measurement tradition (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005; Edwards, 1994, 2002). SNT contributes a specific account of the personal characteristics around which sorting occurs, a neural hypothesis for part of that structure, and predictions that distinguish fit from generally favorable personal traits or environments. The three dimensions and the proposed matching rule must earn their place through predictive performance.

## 2. The Three Dimensions

| Dimension | Construct | Proposed relevance to sorting and fit |
|---|---|---|
| **g — General intelligence** | Capacity to learn, reason, and handle complexity | Intellectual pace, depth of conversation, and task complexity |
| **o — Cognitive orientation** | Impersonal-mechanistic ↔ social-relational framing of meaning and interaction | Expected conversational content, communicative purpose, interpretation, and shared interests |
| **c — Cooperativeness** | Willingness to act honestly, reciprocate, and sustain shared commitments; the individual-level disposition behind generalized trust | Compatible expectations about obligations, reciprocity, and personal advantage |

SNT selects these dimensions to represent three distinct sources of coordination difficulty: differences in the complexity people can engage with, the terms on which they expect mutual understanding to occur, and their willingness to cooperate. Understanding the same complexity, interpreting an exchange on the same terms, and dealing honestly with one another are different achievements. This mechanism-based rationale nominates the axes; observed assortment supports their relevance, and joint prediction tests their sufficiency. The set is a minimal core, not an exhaustive taxonomy. A redundant dimension would be cut or replaced; an additional dimension must improve held-out prediction of sorting or fit beyond adequately measured g/o/c and encounter structure.

### 2.1 General intelligence

The g coordinate is anchored by the psychometric construct of general intelligence, for which IQ is currently the best available measure. Partner IQ correlations average approximately .44 (Horwitz et al., 2023), and intelligence is associated with occupational complexity (Gottfredson, 1997). SNT predicts that intellectual similarity helps sustain a shared pace and depth of thought, while matching demands to ability supports learning and engagement. The neural account treats intelligence as capacity available across forms of cognition.

### 2.2 Cognitive orientation

Orientation describes the impersonal-mechanistic versus social-relational terms on which a person habitually interprets and communicates. Its interpersonal expression concerns three expectations: what content belongs in an exchange, what the communication is meant to accomplish, and how meaning should be inferred. An exchange can center on explicit claims and practical problems, or on intentions, relationships, and what the act of speaking conveys between participants. Both forms occur within a person; the proposed coordinate describes their relative habitual emphasis, not an inability to use the other.

The immediate source of friction is a mismatch between the expectations used to produce and interpret a message. A correction offered as useful information can be received as an assertion of superiority; reassurance offered as care can be received as evasion. These are illustrative mismatches, not diagnostic rules. Neither requires low intelligence or bad intentions. SNT predicts that alignment in communicative expectations supports sustained mutual understanding and voluntary association.

SNT further predicts that these expectations and people–things interests express a common orientation. That connection must be tested by measuring them separately, rather than defining one as the other. A person can be interested in both people and things or neither; a single relative coordinate must therefore be compared with separate interest dimensions (Graziano et al., 2011). Existing couple evidence supports the interest-assortment component: partners show modest-to-moderate similarity on Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, and Social interests and moderate similarity on full interest profiles, attributable to active initial assortment rather than convergence or social homogamy, and remaining after accounting for Big Five personality (Banov et al., 2023). Both orientations occur in both sexes; on sex-typed interests, however, the sexes show one of the largest psychological differences (Stewart-Williams & Halsey, 2021).

SNT proposes sexual selection as an evolutionary account. The male–female distinction itself originated when selection split a once-uniform population of gamete-producers into two complementary specialists—many small gametes competing and few large gametes investing (Parker, Baker & Smith, 1972)—and across mammals the higher-investing sex specializes in parental effort, the lower in mating effort and competition (Trivers, 1972). The proposed human extension is that pair bonding, mutual mate choice, and an ancestral division of labor (Stewart-Williams & Thomas, 2013) favored complementary things- and people-oriented specializations. The measured interest difference is substantial—d ≈ .93 on the Things–People dimension (Su et al., 2009)—but its size does not by itself establish that evolutionary explanation. The distributions overlap heavily.

Direct couple measurements provide evidence of interest-profile similarity: randomly paired men and women show anti-correlated interest profiles (−.18 average profile correlation) because of the mean sex difference alone, whereas spouses in 1,231 directly measured couples show positively correlated profiles (+.18)—partners resemble one another in interest shape beyond what the population sex gap produces by chance (Etzel et al., 2019).

Within a sex, SNT predicts assortment by orientation similarity. Between the sexes, the complementary-niche account proposes matching on standing within each partner's own sex rather than equal raw levels. At equal percentiles, raw differences follow the separation of the two distributions at that percentile; they need not be constant. This rule must be compared with raw-distance matching under the same available-partner distributions. Mean couple gaps alone cannot distinguish the rules: the mean difference is fixed by the partners' marginal distributions, even if pairings are shuffled. A discriminating test therefore requires pairing-sensitive predictions of actual partner choice or subsequent outcomes, with reference distributions and meeting opportunities specified independently (Section 6.1).

A descriptive analysis of American Community Survey microdata (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024) links 115,382 married employed couples, ages 25–65, to occupational Realistic-minus-Social profiles from O*NET 29.1 (National Center for O*NET Development, 2024). The unweighted partner correlation is +.14. This is occupational-proxy assortment, not a direct estimate of interest assortment: occupations incorporate opportunity, education, and labor-market constraints. The mean couple gap and mean within-sex percentile difference do not identify the matching rule, and existing couples cannot separate initial choice from convergence or survival selection. The proposed percentile rule remains untested.

Communication measures should distinguish expected content, communicative purpose, and interpretation from surface style. A short message can rely heavily on shared relational context; an elaborate message can remain entirely impersonal. Directness, brevity, warmth, and politeness are therefore not orientation scores. The battery's elicited-message production and communication choices (Section 5) explore candidate indicators of what participants choose to convey; testing how they interpret others' messages requires separate measures. Across these measures, habitual expectations must be distinguished from learned conventions, relationship familiarity, and adaptation to the immediate situation.

The proposed cognitive mechanism is relative ease in mechanistic versus social-relational processing. This is an explanation to test, not the definition of orientation: communicative mismatch does not by itself establish an attentional or neural cause. Jack et al. (2013) found reciprocal activation and suppression between reasoning about mental states and reasoning about causal-mechanical properties, and the task-positive/default-mode distinction supplies the mechanistic starting point (Menon, 2023); the poles also have dedicated machinery — a parietal-frontal system that runs forward simulations of physical dynamics (Fischer et al., 2016) and a default network that, at rest, consolidates newly learned social information and primes subsequent social reasoning (Spunt et al., 2015; Meyer et al., 2019). Repeatable neural measurements must establish whether this balance predicts independently measured orientation.

### 2.3 Cooperativeness

Cooperativeness describes willingness to participate in honest, reciprocal cooperation and accept mutual obligations. SNT predicts sorting around compatible expectations of commitment and personal advantage. Two people can share intelligence and interests yet experience persistent friction over what they owe each other. The construct is willingness to deal honestly, not trustingness: a cooperative person can be cautious about whom to trust.

Trust research supports the same reading from outside the personality tradition. Generalized-trust items ("most people can be trusted") predict trustworthy behavior in economic games much better than trusting behavior (Glaeser et al., 2000)—the survey answer tracks the disposition, not only a belief about others—and both trusting and trustworthy choices are partly heritable (Cesarini et al., 2008), consistent with a common disposition behind the two ends of an exchange. The battery therefore pairs its dark-triad items with a short generalized-trust scale (Section 5); the central measurement prediction is that the trust items converge with the dark-triad score and remain independent of intelligence and orientation.

Assortment for antisocial behavior, Dark Triad traits, and Honesty–Humility motivates this dimension (Krueger et al., 1998; Richards et al., 2024). In 451 Dutch married couples, actual spouse similarity was .39 for Honesty–Humility (Lee et al., 2026).

The HEXACO Honesty–Humility factor is the closest established trait family to c: its sincerity, fairness, and greed-avoidance facets concern genuine, non-exploitative dealing, and the factor is a consistent predictor of prosocial behavior (Ashton & Lee, 2007; Thielmann et al., 2020). The mapping is partial—the remaining modesty facet concerns self-importance rather than cooperation—so Honesty–Humility anchors the measurement and assortment case for c without defining it.

### 2.4 From person profiles to environments

Each dimension suggests a practical placement question:

- **Intelligence:** Do the work and surrounding peers support an appropriate pace and level of complexity?
- **Orientation:** Do activities fit the person's interests, and do participants share expectations about conversational content, purpose, and interpretation?
- **Cooperativeness:** Are expectations about honesty, reciprocity, and commitment mutually understood and compatible?

These questions turn the dimensions from person descriptions into testable placement claims, taken up in Section 4. The person profile is distinct from learned coordination conventions: communication norms, authority expectations, and routines can create friction between similar profiles or support coordination between different ones.

## 3. Assortment as the Starting Evidence

### 3.1 People select similar partners and peers

Observed partner correlations describe actual sorting. Horwitz et al. (2023) report meta-analytic correlations of approximately .44 for IQ scores, .58 for political values, and .56 for religiosity. Education, religion, and politics are useful benchmarks because they combine personal characteristics with social institutions and opportunities to meet, making them the harder test for compression. Occupational personality profiles replicate across self- and informant reports (Anni et al., 2025).

SNT's **core prediction** is that g/o/c explains sorting and fit across settings. Intelligence indexes shared complexity; interests and communication reflect orientation; honesty and reciprocal commitment reflect cooperativeness. Its stronger **compression hypothesis** predicts little residual partner assortment across reliable psychological traits after accounting for this profile and meeting opportunities. Persistent residual sorting limits the core's completeness; it does not erase its predictive contribution.

The proposed causal order places g/o/c upstream of religious and political dispositions. In particular, SNT predicts that cognitive orientation contributes to conservatism, while the joint profile helps explain both conservatism and religiosity. These attitudes can then influence institutional membership and partner selection. A recognizable religion–conservatism cluster can therefore be a downstream expression of the proposed axes rather than an additional fundamental axis. This is a causal hypothesis, not a conclusion obtainable from factor loadings alone; social context and feedback from institutions remain possible contributors.

That is a substantive prediction. It requires measuring both partners independently and testing whether other traits retain cross-partner associations after accounting for g/o/c, shared institutions, and measurement error. Persistent residual assortment would identify something the model leaves out. Preference, social homogamy, and convergence can all contribute to observed similarity; longitudinal data can help separate them. Partner correlations based on questionnaires also retain those questionnaires' measurement limits, which makes independently tested intelligence a particularly useful anchor. For the orientation dimension between the sexes, SNT additionally predicts percentile-structured rather than raw-scored assortment (Section 2.2) — a refinement that standard partner-correlation estimates alone cannot establish.

### 3.2 Observed associations and the case for distinct dimensions

The empirical case requires two kinds of evidence: associations among traits within people, and resemblance between partners. Neither substitutes for the other. The following comparison uses observed associations without filling missing correlations or constructing a synthetic factor solution.

Holtrop, Born, and de Vries (2015) jointly measured the Personal Globe Inventory's people–things axis and HEXACO personality in 656 Dutch participants. Table 1 reproduces the complete six-domain personality row for that axis. Higher orientation scores indicate people rather than things interests.

**Table 1.** Observed correlations with people–things orientation (Holtrop et al., 2015, Table 3).

| Personality domain | Correlation with people-oriented interests |
|---|---:|
| Honesty–Humility | .18 |
| Emotionality | .40 |
| Extraversion | .24 |
| Agreeableness | −.02 |
| Conscientiousness | .09 |
| Openness to experience | .04 |

The modest association with Honesty–Humility supports distinguishing people-oriented interests from the trait family closest to c; it does not establish latent independence or equate either instrument with SNT's full construct. The stronger Emotionality association also shows why orientation cannot simply be presumed independent of neighboring traits. These are pooled, unadjusted correlations: sex correlates .59 with people-oriented interests and .23 with Honesty–Humility in the same table (male = 1, female = 2). Within-sex relationships therefore need separate examination. The sample combines five groups and overrepresents higher education and social-domain work or study. The interest inventory also incorporates self-rated competence, not just activity liking; it is not an objective ability measure.

Partner evidence supplies a different part of the argument. IQ shows substantial partner resemblance (approximately .44; Horwitz et al., 2023), Honesty–Humility shows resemblance in directly measured couples (.39; Lee et al., 2026), and interest studies find partner similarity beyond broad personality (Banov et al., 2023). Etzel et al.'s (2019) +.18 is similarity across interest profiles within couples, not a cross-partner correlation on a single orientation score, and should not be ranked directly against the IQ and Honesty–Humility coefficients. These findings motivate measuring intelligence, orientation, and cooperativeness together; they do not establish that each contributes independent sorting information in the same couples.

A joint structural test remains necessary. It requires a complete observed matrix with multiple suitable indicators for each axis, cognitive performance rather than self-rated competence, and a separate test of cross-partner resemblance. The studies summarized here are not combined into an artificial common sample. No joint g/o/c factor solution or percentage of partner assortment explained is claimed.

The upstream hypothesis also remains distinct from descriptive factor structure. Conservatism and religiosity may form a recognizable cluster while partly expressing g/o/c and influencing institutional membership downstream (Section 3.1). Testing that account requires both attitudes and the three axes in the same sample, followed by comparisons of residual attitude covariance and partner assortment (Section 6.1). A separate attitude factor alone cannot determine whether it represents another fundamental axis or downstream organization of the proposed profile.

### 3.3 Sorting and relationship outcomes require different measurements

Partner assortment is the primary evidence for selecting SNT's dimensions. Relationship satisfaction is a more indirect test because it combines relationship experiences with each respondent's dispositions and reporting style. Personality—including neuroticism—is associated with relationship satisfaction, including the satisfaction reported by the person's partner (Malouff et al., 2010). Such associations can reflect actual interpersonal effects as well as differences in appraisal; controlling reporting bias requires more than assuming all dissatisfaction is noise.

Broad personality-profile similarity has not robustly predicted satisfaction in some large couple studies (Weidmann et al., 2023). This constrains those measures and outcomes without directly testing a behaviorally measured g/o/c profile. Equally, a new measurement proposal must demonstrate its advantage rather than presume that unfamiliar tasks are reliable.

SNT therefore prioritizes independently assessed partner traits, observed affiliation, continued voluntary interaction, and prospective relationship formation and stability. Satisfaction can supply additional information when measured from both partners and separated from their individual dispositions. Brief first-meeting attraction is narrower still—a preview of association, not the sustained coordination the theory aims to explain.

### 3.4 Candidate compatibility function

If partners and peers are sorted by profile similarity, the simplest candidate compatibility function is:

C(i,j) = exp(−Δg² − Δo² − Δc²)

The differences are between-person distances on scales fixed before evaluation. Each dimension contributes a separate penalty; matching on one does not erase a mismatch on another. The equal weights and Gaussian shape are modeling choices to compare with alternatives. For between-sex matches, whether the orientation distance is computed on raw or within-sex-standardized scores (Section 2.2) is an open modeling choice the planned comparisons should include.

The score describes proposed fit, not absolute relationship quality. Two equally uncooperative people receive the same zero c-distance as two equally cooperative people; their outcomes may differ substantially through trait-level effects. Tests must therefore separate the benefits of each partner's cooperativeness from any additional benefit of similarity, including both partners' levels as well as their differences. The model also leaves room to test asymmetric accommodation and complementary rather than similar strategies.

## 4. Placement Evidence: Objective Outcomes

Educational differentiation offers objective outcomes and interventions that change placement. The anchor evidence is a second-order meta-analysis of a century of research (Steenbergen-Hu et al., 2016): within-class ability grouping benefits achievement (g = .19–.30, where g denotes standardized effect sizes, not the intelligence coordinate) and accelerated gifted learners outperform nonaccelerated same-age peers by .70 standardized units with no detectable difference from older peers (.09), whereas ordinary between-class grouping shows little average effect (.04–.06). The lesson is specificity: placement helps when it changes the task, the pace, or the peer group in a way that fits the learner; mere reshuffling does not.

The strongest causal evidence makes the same point. In a randomized experiment across 121 Kenyan primary schools, tracking pupils into classes by prior achievement benefited pupils at all initial achievement levels, because teachers taught to the class's level (Duflo et al., 2011). In a Dutch university experiment that randomized tutorial-group composition, a model fitted to the assignment implied that three-way tracking would raise low- and medium-ability students' achievement by .19 standardized units and cut dropout by 12 percentage points, with no detected effect on high-ability students (Booij et al., 2017).

For SNT, these results establish that placement causally matters and that its effects run through matching task difficulty and peer composition to the learner—not through across-the-board gains. The discriminating experiment separates **task fit** from **peer fit**: vary instructional demand while holding peers comparable, or vary peer composition while holding instruction comparable. Existing educational results do not yet validate the full g/o/c matching rule; they justify testing it.

## 5. Measurement: An Implemented Pilot

To measure the three dimensions, the pilot battery draws on established measurement traditions and techniques. For g, it uses objectively scored, untimed randomized Sandia matrix problems, forward digit span, and symbol-matching speed tasks. Their task families have established psychometric histories; the particular pilot schedule and randomized matrix forms require their own validation. For o, it records self-paced viewing of social versus mechanical images, elicited messages serving coordination, refusal, and misunderstanding-resolution functions, choices among truthful communication strategies, and people-versus-things questionnaires. The message and communication-choice probes remain unscored pending validation; practical content, recipient modeling, and relational packaging are candidate indicators rather than established orientation scores. For c, it uses three self-report instruments: an Honesty–Humility–inspired scale covering sincerity, fairness, and greed avoidance (Section 2.3), reverse-scored Dark Triad items, and a short generalized-trust scale.

Across all three axes the battery follows one design principle: sample each dimension from more than one kind of evidence, and limit dependence on self-description—the cheapest source but also the most rehearsed. Hence the evidence hierarchy: objective performance, elicited behavior, hypothetical choices, and only then questionnaires. These classes are psychometrically different—performance has correct answers, elicited behavior can be observed, hypothetical choices and questionnaires report on the self—and they should remain distinguishable during validation rather than being blended into composites prematurely.

The recorded unit is task-level evidence; axis-level scores and normative percentiles live at the scoring layer above it, with definitions fixed in advance of validation. All three cooperativeness measures are self-report—retained together deliberately, since a pilot can afford redundant indicators and the choice between them should follow data—so reliance on self-description varies across axes rather than disappearing uniformly.

Validation must establish reliability, test–retest stability, construct coverage, and incremental prediction, with the known confounds guarded explicitly: orientation indicators must be separated from intelligence, literacy, and stimulus salience; reverse-scored dark-trait measures cannot by themselves establish willingness to trust or cooperate; and any eventual composite must show that its indicators converge on the intended construct without discarding genuine differences between them.

## 6. Predictions and Research Design

### 6.1 Core tests

1. **Three-dimensional core.** Independent indicators distinguish intelligence, orientation, and cooperativeness. Compare g/o/c with simpler and richer models. Redundancy calls for reduction; reproducible incremental prediction calls for extension. Candidate residuals include uncertainty regulation, the scope of cooperative obligations, and authority preferences—not additional axes unless they pass that test.

2. **Observed assortment.** Partners, friends, and voluntarily formed groups resemble one another on the measured profile beyond what meeting opportunities predict. Use independent assessments rather than one person rating both members of a pair. For orientation between the sexes, compare raw-distance and within-sex-relative matching models using reliable direct measurements and independently specified reference distributions (Section 2.2). Evaluate held-out predictions of observed pairings against shuffled pairings within plausible meeting pools, retaining the same marginal distributions. Prospective measurement before pairing helps separate choice from convergence and survival selection. Sample-size requirements should follow simulations of these competing models rather than tests of mean couple gaps.

3. **Compression and upstream explanation of other sorting markers.** Accounting for g/o/c substantially reduces cross-partner associations in other reliable psychological traits. Measure conservatism and religiosity alongside independent, multi-indicator g/o/c measures in both partners. First test whether orientation predicts conservatism beyond g and c and whether the joint profile explains religious and political dispositions. Then test how much attitude covariance and cross-partner resemblance remain after accounting for the profile, measurement error, social background, and meeting opportunities. Compare models with and without additional attitude-specific structure by held-out prediction. Treat institutional membership as a possible mediator as well as a source of meeting opportunities: report models before and after conditioning on it, rather than automatically removing pathways the theory proposes. Longitudinal measurements and explicit alternative causal models are needed to assess direction; cross-sectional mediation or heritability alone would not establish it. Substantial, replicable residual sorting would limit the strong compression claim, without by itself establishing a new fundamental axis. Traits that supply the battery's own cooperativeness items, notably Honesty–Humility, are convergent-measurement checks rather than independent compression targets; the cleaner compression tests use traits the battery does not measure, such as antisocial behavior.

4. **Fit beyond trait levels.** Profile distances predict sustained interaction, coordination, and relationship outcomes beyond actor and partner effects. Compare the Gaussian equal-weight rule with alternative weights, shapes, and complementarity. Use response-surface methods where appropriate to distinguish matching from generally beneficial levels (Edwards, 1994, 2002; Humberg et al., 2019).

5. **Placement improves outcomes.** Assigning tasks or peers using measured profiles improves performance, persistence, or functioning relative to equally resourced alternatives. Separate instructional changes from peer changes and record who benefits or loses.

6. **Neural mapping.** Repeatable capacity and orientation indicators have distinguishable relationships with cognitive performance and network engagement. The account fails if the proposed neural markers are unreliable, nonspecific, or add no explanatory value beyond simpler measures.

### 6.2 Evaluation priorities

Start with reliable person-level measurement, then paired and group data. Weak self-report associations can reflect weak signal, identity bias, or reference-group differences. Resolve these alternatives through indicators with different error sources and explicit measurement-error models. Repeated null results across reliable, independently validated measures count against the proposed pathway. A collection of plausible tasks is not yet a psychometric instrument. Predefine scoring, scale direction, exclusions, meaningful effect thresholds, and the primary outcomes before testing prediction in held-out samples.

For assortment, use independently measured established couples and peer groups, with longitudinal observations where possible. For compatibility, measure actual interactions and follow relationships over time. Include both partners' traits, prior relationship duration, available alternatives, and reporting dispositions where relevant. Existing-couple samples alone cannot identify initial partner choice or distinguish compatibility from survival selection.

For placement, prioritize randomized or credible quasi-experimental changes with objective outcomes. A useful model should improve decisions, not merely describe differences after they occur. Report nulls alongside gains and compare against simpler placement rules.

### 6.3 Profiles and types

The primary output is a continuous person profile. Recurring combinations could support descriptive types if they replicate and improve practical prediction beyond continuous scores; clusters alone do not establish natural categories (Bauer & Curran, 2003).

## 7. Conclusion

SNT proposes that general intelligence, cognitive orientation, and cooperativeness organize where people thrive and with whom they fit. Partner assortment anchors the sorting question; educational interventions show that changing placement can improve performance. The proposed neural account and low-self-report battery aim to connect these observations through a compact, measurable profile.

The decisive test is whether g/o/c explains observed sorting and improves placement better than existing markers and simpler alternatives. The aim is to measure the person well enough to help them find people, activities, and environments in which they can thrive.
